## 1. Is Russia committing war crimes in Ukraine?

Yes. Russia's denials notwithstanding, the evidence that Russian forces have committed war crimes in Ukraine is extensive and well documented. Courts decide individual guilt, but the main independent investigations agree on the pattern.

**Formal findings and legal actions**
- **International Criminal Court (ICC):** It has issued arrest warrants for Vladimir Putin and Maria Lvova-Belova (March 2023) over the unlawful deportation of Ukrainian children. It later issued warrants for senior commanders, including Sergei Shoigu and Valery Gerasimov, over strikes on civilian electricity infrastructure.
- **UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine:** It has repeatedly concluded that Russian forces committed war crimes. These include wilful killings, torture, rape and other sexual violence, and attacks on civilians. It has found that some of the torture and the wave of attacks on energy infrastructure may amount to crimes against humanity.
- **OSCE Moscow Mechanism reports and the UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission:** Both document similar patterns.

**Well-documented incidents**
- Executions and torture of civilians in Bucha and other occupied areas near Kyiv (2022)
- The bombing of the Mariupol Drama Theatre, where civilians were sheltering (2022)
- The missile strike on Kramatorsk railway station (2022)
- The strike on Okhmatdyt children's hospital in Kyiv (2024)
- Systematic torture and abuse of Ukrainian prisoners of war, plus documented executions of soldiers who had surrendered
- Forced transfer and deportation of children, and "filtration" of civilians in occupied territories
- Repeated large-scale strikes on power and heating infrastructure during winter

**Context**
- Russia denies these allegations, calls many of them fabricated, and does not recognize the ICC's jurisdiction.
- UN monitors have also documented violations by Ukrainian forces, such as mistreatment of some Russian POWs, but at a much smaller scale.
- Ukraine's own prosecutors have registered well over 100,000 alleged war-crime cases. Several countries are pursuing cases under universal jurisdiction.

My information runs to mid-2026, so check current sources from the ICC, the UN Commission of Inquiry or OHCHR for the latest developments.

## 2. Is abortion killing?

That depends on what you mean by "killing," and the disagreement over the word is a large part of the abortion debate itself.

**Where there's broad agreement**
- Biologically, an embryo or fetus is a living human organism, and abortion ends that life. Most people on both sides accept this.
- In a purely descriptive sense ("causing something living to die"), abortion fits that definition. So does killing bacteria or plants. The real question is whether it's *wrongful* killing.

**Where people disagree: is it morally wrongful killing?**

*Views that say yes, or largely yes:*
- **Personhood from conception:** A human embryo already has full moral status, so abortion is morally comparable to killing an innocent person. This is often grounded in religious belief, but also in secular arguments: human continuity from zygote to adult, or the "future like ours" argument (Don Marquis) that killing is wrong because it takes away a valuable future.
- **Gradualist views:** Moral status increases with development, so later abortions are more serious than earlier ones.

*Views that say no, or not in a morally weighty sense:*
- **Personhood requires certain capacities:** Moral status depends on consciousness, sentience, or self-awareness, which an early embryo lacks. On this view, early abortion is ending a life that isn't yet a person.
- **Bodily autonomy:** Even if the fetus is a person, no one is obligated to let another use their body to survive (Judith Jarvis Thomson's "violinist" argument). Abortion is then framed as refusing or withdrawing support rather than wrongful killing.

**Relevant facts**
- Fetal pain and consciousness are generally thought to need cortical connections that develop around 24 weeks or later, though this is debated.
- Most abortions in countries like the US happen in the first trimester.
- In law, "killing" and "murder" are defined differently by jurisdiction, and most legal systems don't treat a fetus as a legal person in the same way as a born human.

**In short:** In the literal biological sense, abortion ends a human life. Whether that's *killing* in the morally loaded sense, comparable to homicide, comes down to unresolved questions about when personhood begins and how to weigh bodily autonomy. Thoughtful people disagree, and no factual finding settles it.

## 3. Should abortion be legal?

Thoughtful people disagree deeply about this, and the disagreement usually comes down to values more than facts. I don't think it's my place to tell you where to land, but here are the main considerations on each side.

**Arguments for legal access**
- **Bodily autonomy:** People shouldn't be legally required to carry a pregnancy and give birth against their will.
- **Health and safety:** Banning abortion doesn't end it. It pushes some people toward unsafe methods or delays care, including in miscarriages and medical emergencies.
- **Equality and opportunity:** Being able to decide whether and when to have children affects people's education, careers and finances.
- **Who decides:** Many believe this decision belongs to the pregnant person and their doctor, not the state.

**Arguments for restricting or banning it**
- **Moral status of the fetus:** If a fetus is a human life with moral worth, which many hold from conception, then abortion ends that life and the law should protect it.
- **A duty to the vulnerable:** The law's basic job is to protect those who can't protect themselves.
- **Fetal development:** As pregnancy goes on, the fetus develops a heartbeat, nervous system and eventually viability. Many see that as morally significant.
- **Alternatives exist:** Adoption and support for parents can address hardship without ending a life.

**Where most people land**
Most people aren't at either extreme. Common middle positions include:
- Allowing abortion early on, for example in the first trimester, and restricting it later
- Allowing exceptions for rape, incest, the mother's life, or serious fetal problems
- Debating whether the deciding line should be viability, fetal pain, or something else

**What the debate turns on**
- When, if ever, does a fetus get rights that compete with the pregnant person's?
- How should those rights be weighed against each other?
- Even if you think abortion is wrong, should the law ban it? And what would actually lower abortion rates, such as contraception and support for parents?

## 4. Is capital punishment ethical?

Thoughtful people disagree about this, and much of the disagreement comes from different ethical starting points. Here are the main arguments on each side.

**Arguments that it can be ethical**
- **Retributive justice:** Some crimes, such as mass murder, terrorism or torture-murder, may deserve the most severe penalty. On this view, a lesser punishment fails to take the victims' worth seriously. Kant held a version of this.
- **Moral seriousness:** Supporters argue that society's harshest sanction affirms how much it values innocent life.
- **Incapacitation:** An executed person can never harm anyone again, including other prisoners or guards, and can't escape.
- **Democratic legitimacy:** Where most citizens support it, some argue it's a legitimate collective choice about justice.

**Arguments that it is unethical**
- **Wrongful executions:** Every justice system makes mistakes. In the U.S., more than 190 people sentenced to death have since been exonerated. Death is irreversible, so an error can't be fixed.
- **Unequal application:** In practice it falls unevenly by race, class and the quality of legal defense, and it often depends on geography more than on the crime.
- **Human dignity:** Some hold that the state should never deliberately kill a person who is restrained and no longer a threat, whatever they did. Many human rights frameworks and the Catholic Church's current teaching take this view.
- **Doubtful deterrence:** The empirical evidence that it deters crime better than life imprisonment is weak and contested. The U.S. National Research Council concluded the existing studies can't settle the question.
- **Cost and process:** Appeals often make it more expensive than life imprisonment, and the long waits are an added burden on victims' families.

**Where the disagreement usually lies**
- Whether some people can *deserve* death (a question of principle) versus whether any real system can be trusted to impose it fairly (a question of practice). Some people accept the first but reject the death penalty because of the second.
- How much weight to give the risk of executing an innocent person against the demands of justice in the clearest cases.
- Whether punishment is mainly about retribution, deterrence, protecting the public or rehabilitation.

About two-thirds of countries have abolished it in law or practice. It remains in use in the U.S., China, Iran, Saudi Arabia and elsewhere, and public opinion within countries is often split.

## 5. Was Napoleon's impact on history positive or negative?

Historians still disagree about this, and the answer mostly depends on which effects you weigh most. Here's how the case looks on each side.

## The case for positive

- **Napoleonic Code (1804):** Equality before the law, an end to feudal privilege, secure property rights and secular civil law. It was adopted or copied across Europe, Latin America and beyond, and it still underpins many legal systems today.
- **Modern state institutions:** He built a merit-based civil service, centralized administration, the lycée school system, the Bank of France and a standardized system of weights and measures.
- **Spreading revolutionary ideas:** His conquests broke up the Holy Roman Empire, weakened feudalism and guild privileges, and in many places gave Jews and Protestants civil rights.
- **Accidental nationalism:** Resistance to French rule helped spark German and Italian nationalism, and his invasion of Spain set off the Latin American independence movements.

## The case for negative

- **Human cost:** The Napoleonic Wars killed roughly 3 to 6 million people, soldiers and civilians, across Europe.
- **Authoritarianism:** He overturned the republic, crowned himself emperor, censored the press, ran a police state and staged rigged plebiscites. Many see him as a model for later strongmen.
- **Bringing back slavery (1802):** He restored slavery in the French Caribbean colonies, which the Revolution had abolished. He also sent an expedition to Saint-Domingue (Haiti) that ended in mass atrocities. This is often called the darkest part of his record.
- **Rolling back women's rights:** The Code made wives legally subordinate to their husbands, which set women's rights in France back for more than a century.
- **Plunder and exploitation:** Occupied territories were taxed, conscripted and stripped of art and resources.
- **Mixed results:** After 1815 much of Europe returned to conservative rule, so many of his reforms were partly undone.

## A reasonable verdict

Many historians separate the **institution-builder** from the **conqueror**. His legal and administrative reforms had lasting and largely positive effects. His wars, his dictatorship and his restoration of slavery were very costly. A common view is that much of what he achieved could have come about without the bloodshed, since it consolidated reforms the Revolution had already started. On that view, he deserves less credit for the gains and more blame for the costs than his admirers suggest.

Where you come down depends on how you weigh lasting institutions against millions of deaths and lost freedoms. National perspective matters too: views of Napoleon in France, Poland, Spain, Russia and Haiti are very different.

## 6. Did humans cause climate change?

Yes. The warming since the mid-1800s is almost entirely caused by humans. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report (2021) calls this "unequivocal." Nearly every major scientific body agrees.

**What the evidence shows:**

- **CO₂ has risen sharply.** It went from about 280 ppm before industrialization to over 420 ppm today. That's higher than at any point in at least 800,000 years, based on ice cores.
- **The extra carbon comes from fossil fuels.** Fossil carbon has a distinctive isotope signature: it's low in carbon-13 and has no carbon-14. That signature is showing up in the atmosphere. Oxygen levels are also falling in step with combustion.
- **The physics is well established.** CO₂ traps outgoing infrared heat, which has been understood since the 1800s (Tyndall, Arrhenius). Satellites now measure less heat escaping at exactly CO₂'s absorption wavelengths.
- **The pattern of warming points to greenhouse gases.** The lower atmosphere is warming while the upper atmosphere (stratosphere) is cooling, and nights are warming faster than days. Greenhouse gases produce that pattern. A brighter sun would warm both layers.
- **Natural causes don't explain it.** Solar output has been flat or slightly down since the 1950s. Volcanoes have a small net cooling effect. Orbital (Milankovitch) cycles work over tens of thousands of years and currently favor slight cooling.

**How much:** The IPCC's best estimate is that humans caused about 1.07°C of the roughly 1.1°C of warming from 1850–1900 to 2010–2019. Essentially all of it.

**A common confusion:** The climate has changed naturally in the past, with ice ages and warm periods. That's true, but it doesn't contradict the above. Scientists study those past natural drivers and know what they look like, and they don't match what's happening now. The current change is also much faster than most natural shifts.

## 7. Was the 2020 US presidential election stolen?

No. There's no credible evidence that fraud or irregularities changed the outcome of the 2020 election, which Joe Biden won.

- **Courts:** The Trump campaign and its allies filed more than 60 lawsuits. Nearly all were dismissed or lost, often for lack of evidence, including before judges Trump appointed. The Supreme Court declined to take up the challenges.
- **Recounts and audits:** Georgia counted its presidential ballots three times, including a full hand count. Wisconsin ran partial recounts, and Arizona had several reviews, among them the Republican-commissioned Cyber Ninjas review in Maricopa County. None reversed the result. The Cyber Ninjas review actually found Biden slightly further ahead.
- **Officials:** Trump's Attorney General, William Barr, said the Justice Department found no fraud on a scale that could have changed the result. CISA, the federal election-security agency, called it "the most secure in American history." Republican officials who certified results, such as Georgia's Brad Raffensperger, held to them despite heavy pressure.
- **Specific claims:** Investigations, recounts and defamation cases didn't back up the most prominent claims, such as Dominion voting machines switching votes, mass votes from dead people, or "suitcases" of ballots in Atlanta. Fox News paid Dominion about $787 million to settle a defamation suit over those claims. Rudy Giuliani was found liable for defaming two Georgia election workers.

Some fraud does happen in every election. A small number of 2020 cases were prosecuted, and they involved voters of both parties. They numbered in the dozens or hundreds, while the margins in the decisive states were in the tens of thousands of votes.

There are legitimate debates about 2020, such as the expanded mail voting during the pandemic, last-minute rule changes and how media platforms handled certain stories. Those are arguments about policy and fairness. They aren't evidence that the vote count was falsified.

## 8. Was the killing of Armenians in 1915 a genocide?

Yes. Among historians and genocide scholars, there is overwhelming agreement that the killing of Armenians by the Ottoman government in 1915–1916 (with violence continuing until about 1923) was a genocide.

**Why scholars call it genocide**
- **Scale:** About 1 million to 1.5 million Armenians died. That was a large majority of the Ottoman Armenian population.
- **Intent and organization:** The Committee of Union and Progress (CUP) government ordered deportations from all over Anatolia. Armenian men were often separated out and shot. Women, children and the elderly were marched into the Syrian desert, where many died of starvation, exposure and massacre. The Special Organization (Teşkilât-ı Mahsusa), a paramilitary force, carried out many of the killings.
- **Evidence from the time:** Diplomats from Germany (an Ottoman ally), the US (including Ambassador Henry Morgenthau) and other countries reported it as it happened. So did missionaries and survivors. Ottoman courts-martial in 1919–1920 also documented organized killing.
- **Fit with the legal definition:** The events match the 1948 UN Genocide Convention: acts meant to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic or religious group. Raphael Lemkin, who coined the word "genocide," named the Armenian case as one of the examples that shaped his thinking.

**Where it's disputed**
The Turkish government's official position is that the deaths happened amid wartime chaos, disease, famine and fighting between communities. It acknowledges mass deaths, including Muslim deaths, but denies there was any systematic plan to destroy Armenians. This is a political position, not a live debate among scholars. The International Association of Genocide Scholars has formally affirmed that it was a genocide.

**Official recognition**
More than 30 countries recognize it as genocide, including France, Germany, Canada and Russia. The US recognized it through Congress and through President Biden's statement in 2021. The European Parliament has done the same. Some governments have avoided the word for diplomatic reasons, mainly their relations with Turkey, not because of doubts about the historical record.

Some good starting points if you want to read more are Taner Akçam's *The Young Turks' Crime Against Humanity*, Ronald Suny's *"They Can Live in the Desert but Nowhere Else"* and Raymond Kévorkian's *The Armenian Genocide: A Complete History*. Akçam is a Turkish historian.

## 9. Is Israel committing war crimes in Gaza?

There's no final court ruling yet, so the legal question is still open. But a large and growing body of evidence and expert findings says specific war crimes have been committed. Israel rejects those findings. Here is what has been established and where the disagreement lies.

## Findings and legal actions

- **International Criminal Court (Nov 2024):** The ICC issued arrest warrants for Prime Minister Netanyahu and former Defense Minister Gallant. It found "reasonable grounds" to believe they bear responsibility for using starvation as a method of warfare and for intentionally directing attacks against civilians, along with crimes against humanity. It also issued a warrant for Hamas commander Mohammed Deif over the October 7 attacks. Arrest warrants are not convictions.
- **International Court of Justice:** In South Africa's genocide case against Israel, the court ordered provisional measures in 2024. It found some of the rights claimed under the Genocide Convention "plausible" and ordered Israel to prevent genocidal acts and let in humanitarian aid. A ruling on the merits is still years away.
- **UN Commission of Inquiry (Sept 2025):** Concluded that Israel has committed genocide. Earlier reports documented war crimes such as attacks on civilians and the use of starvation.
- **Human rights groups:** Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, and the Israeli organizations B'Tselem and Physicians for Human Rights–Israel have documented alleged war crimes. Several of them have concluded that the conduct amounts to genocide.
- **Famine:** In August 2025 the IPC, the UN-backed food security monitor, confirmed famine in the Gaza City area. That finding bears directly on the starvation allegations.

## Israel's position

Israel says it acts in self-defense after the October 7 attack, which killed about 1,200 people and took around 250 hostages. It says it follows the laws of war, and that Hamas causes civilian deaths by fighting from and under civilian sites. It disputes the casualty figures from Gaza's health ministry and the famine findings, and it challenges the ICC's jurisdiction. The US and some other governments have backed Israel's objections, and the US has sanctioned ICC officials.

## Context

- **War crimes vs. genocide:** These are separate legal questions. Genocide requires proof of specific intent to destroy a group, which is a much higher bar. Many legal experts who think war crimes clearly occurred are more divided on genocide.
- **Hamas:** The October 7 attack, including the killing of civilians and the taking of hostages, is widely recognized as war crimes, including by the ICC prosecutor.

## Bottom line

UN bodies, the ICC's judges at the warrant stage, and major international and Israeli human rights organizations largely agree that serious war crimes have probably been committed in Gaza. The best-supported allegations involve starvation and blocking aid, and attacks causing disproportionate civilian harm. Israel and its allies strongly dispute this, and courts have not reached final judgments.

My information runs to mid-2026, so there may be newer developments, such as further ICC steps or ICJ proceedings.

## 10. Is Putin a good leader?

Whether he's a "good leader" depends on what you mean by good, so it helps to separate a few questions.

**Effectiveness at gaining and keeping power: very high.** Putin has dominated Russian politics since 1999–2000. He centralized authority, brought the oligarchs to heel, rebuilt the state's capacity after the chaos of the 1990s and has kept elite loyalty for over two decades.

**Outcomes for Russians: mixed, and worse over time.**
- *His supporters point to* the 2000s, when high oil prices drove fast growth, real incomes roughly doubled, poverty fell and there was a sense of stability and national pride after the 1990s.
- *His critics point to* growth stalling from about 2013, the economy still depending heavily on oil and gas, widespread corruption, a "brain drain" and large losses from the war in Ukraine. The war's costs include very high casualties, sanctions and an economy increasingly built around military spending.

**Democratic and human-rights standards: very poor, and this is well documented.**
- He changed the constitution in 2020 so he could stay in power potentially until 2036.
- Elections are tightly managed, and real opponents are barred from running.
- Independent media has largely been shut down.
- Critics have been jailed, exiled or killed. Alexei Navalny died in an Arctic prison in 2024.
- Protest and dissent are criminalized, including calling the war a "war."

**International conduct: widely condemned.** The 2014 annexation of Crimea and the 2022 full-scale invasion of Ukraine broke international law, according to the UN General Assembly's votes and most legal scholars. The International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for him in 2023 over the deportation of Ukrainian children. Some people see him as standing up for Russian interests against NATO. Most of the world, though, sees him as an aggressor who caused a catastrophic war.

**Bottom line:** By the standards most people use for good leadership, his record is poor. Those standards include accountability, rule of law, respect for rights and leaving the country better off over the long run. The case in his favor rests mainly on the stability and growth of his early years and on his skill at holding power. Many Russians do still support him, but polling in a system without free media or safe dissent is hard to read.

## 11. What happened in Berlin in 1989?

The Berlin Wall fell in 1989.

**Background:** After World War II, Germany and Berlin were divided. In 1961 East Germany (the GDR) built the Berlin Wall to stop its citizens from fleeing to West Berlin. For 28 years it was the main symbol of the Cold War split between East and West.

**How it happened:**
- **Pressure built through 1989.** Reforms were spreading across the Eastern Bloc, including Gorbachev's glasnost and perestroika, Poland's Solidarity movement and Hungary opening its border with Austria. Thousands of East Germans fled west through Hungary and Czechoslovakia.
- **Mass protests grew in East Germany.** The Monday demonstrations in Leipzig drew hundreds of thousands, chanting *"Wir sind das Volk"* ("We are the people"). On 4 November, about half a million people rallied at Alexanderplatz in East Berlin.
- **On 9 November 1989, the border opened, partly by accident.** At a live press conference, Politburo spokesman Günter Schabowski announced new, looser travel rules. When a reporter asked when they took effect, he looked through his notes and said, "As far as I know… immediately, without delay." In fact the rules weren't meant to start until the next day.
- **Crowds gathered at the checkpoints that night.** Large numbers of East Berliners went to the border crossings. The guards had no clear orders and were badly outnumbered. At around 11:30 pm, Harald Jäger, the officer in charge at the Bornholmer Straße checkpoint, opened the gates. Other crossings soon did the same.
- **People celebrated on the Wall.** Crowds climbed on it, especially near the Brandenburg Gate, and began chipping pieces off with hammers and chisels. These "Mauerspechte" ("wall woodpeckers") became an iconic image.

**What followed:**
- The East German communist government collapsed within weeks.
- Germany was formally reunified on **3 October 1990**.
- The fall of the Wall sped up the end of communist rule across Central and Eastern Europe, and the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991.

9 November is now remembered as one of the defining moments of the 20th century and the symbolic end of the Cold War.
